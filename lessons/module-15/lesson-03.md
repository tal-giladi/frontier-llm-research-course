# 15.3 · Serving cost of architecture choices

Modules 3–5 compared attention designs on quality, training FLOPs and decode memory one model at a time. A serving engine sees something else: hundreds of sequences sharing a GPU, prompts that arrive while others decode, and a fixed number of bytes of HBM to hold every cache. This lesson puts the Stage D model's two phases on the roofline, computes what each Module 3–5 design would cost at 128K tokens on that model's shape, turns cache size into batch capacity and tokens per second, simulates colocated, chunked-prefill and disaggregated serving at stated SLOs, and ends with the place where all of this lands on a research team: the rollout phase of reinforcement learning.

## Why this matters at a frontier lab

Architecture papers report KV-cache savings per token. A serving team needs to know how many users fit on a GPU, how fast each one gets tokens, and what happens to both when a long prompt arrives. A research team running reasoning RL needs the same numbers for a different reason. Lesson 14.3 showed that an RL step is mostly decoding: 256 rollouts of several thousand tokens each, generated by an engine that shares GPUs with the trainer. If the KV cache of one rollout at 32K tokens takes 3.5 GiB, only a dozen fit next to the trainer and the step is a long series of small batches. DeepSeek-V2 motivates MLA with exactly this: a 93.3% smaller KV cache and 5.76× the maximum generation throughput of DeepSeek 67B (abstract). The design choice made in Module 3 is paid for, or repaid, in every rollout of Module 14 and every agent episode of Module 16.

## The idea

### Prefill and decode on the roofline

A model with $N$ parameters stored in $b$ bytes each, on a GPU with peak $P$ FLOP/s and bandwidth $W$ bytes/s (Module 2, `frontierlab.perf.roofline`).

**Prefill** of $T$ prompt tokens costs about $2NT$ FLOPs plus attention, against one read of the weights, $bN$ bytes. Its arithmetic intensity is about $2NT / (bN) = T$ FLOP/byte in BF16, so a prompt longer than the GPU's ridge point ($P/W = 295$ FLOP/byte on an H100) is compute-bound.

**Decode** of one token for each of $B$ sequences at context $S$ costs about $B(2N + a(S))$ FLOPs, where $a(S)$ is the attention term (lesson 01.1: $4 L d_{\text{attn}} S$ per token for full attention), and must read the weights once plus every sequence's cache once:

$$t_{\text{decode}}(B, S) \ge \max\!\Big(\frac{B\,(2N + a(S))}{P},\ \frac{bN + B\,\text{KV}(S)}{W}\Big).$$

At $B = 1$ the intensity is about 1 FLOP/byte: decode is memory-bound by two orders of magnitude, and a step costs the time to read the weights. Raising $B$ adds FLOPs almost for free until the cache reads take over. So $\text{KV}(S)$, the bytes of one sequence's cache, sets two things at once: how many sequences fit next to the weights (**capacity**), and how long a step takes once they do.

### KV bytes for each design

Per token and layer, in elements: $2 \cdot n_{\text{kv}} \cdot d$ for the GQA family (MHA when $n_{\text{kv}}$ equals the query heads, MQA when it is 1); $d_c + d_r$ for MLA (one latent plus the shared RoPE key, DeepSeek-V2 section 2.1); at most $w$ tokens for a sliding-window layer; a fixed $H \cdot d_k \cdot d_v$ state for a linear-attention layer, independent of $S$; and the full keys and values *plus* the indexer keys for DSA, which saves attention FLOPs, not memory (lesson 05.2). The capacity of a GPU with $M$ bytes of HBM, keeping a fraction $r$ free for activations:

$$B_{\max}(S) = \Big\lfloor \frac{(1 - r) M - bN}{\text{KV}(S)} \Big\rfloor.$$

### Scheduling: prefill-first, chunked, disaggregated

Continuous batching (Orca, vLLM) adds and removes sequences at every iteration, and paged KV memory (PagedAttention) lets the engine pack caches with near-zero waste; vLLM reports 2–4× the throughput of FasterTransformer and Orca from this (abstract). What remains is how prefill and decode share a GPU.

- **Prefill-first.** A new prompt is prefilled in the next iteration and every running decode waits for it. An 8K-token prefill of a 1.7B model is tens of milliseconds, so every running user sees one very slow token: a decode stall, which shows in the 90th-percentile time per output token (TPOT).
- **Chunked prefill** (Sarathi-Serve). Each iteration carries all running decodes plus at most a fixed number of prompt tokens, so a long prompt is spread over several iterations and decodes never stall for long. Sarathi-Serve reports up to 2.6× higher serving capacity than vLLM for Mistral-7B on one A100 and up to 5.6× for Falcon-180B with pipeline parallelism (abstract).
- **Disaggregation** (DistServe, Splitwise, Mooncake). Prefill and decode run on different GPUs, and the KV cache produced by prefill is sent to a decode GPU. Each phase can be scaled and parallelised for its own bottleneck: compute for prefill, memory for decode. The price is the transfer ($\text{KV}(P)$ bytes over the link per request) and a fixed split of GPUs between the phases. DistServe reports serving 7.4× more requests or a 12.6× tighter SLO than colocated systems while keeping more than 90% of requests within their latency constraints. Splitwise reports 1.4× higher throughput at 20% lower cost, or 2.35× more throughput at the same cost and power. Mooncake, Kimi's serving system, reports up to 525% more throughput in simulated long-context scenarios and 75% more requests under real workloads (all abstracts; company claims for Mooncake).

**Goodput** is the metric that makes these comparable: the rate of requests that meet *both* a time-to-first-token (TTFT) objective and a TPOT objective (DistServe). A policy can raise throughput and lower goodput at the same time.

### Why this is an RL decision

An RL step generates $G$ responses for each of $Q$ prompts with the current policy. With the trainer colocated, the engine's HBM is what is left after weights, gradients and optimizer state: for a 1.7B model trained in fp32 with AdamW, about $16N = 27.5$ GB, 25.6 GiB. The rollouts run in waves of $B_{\max}(S_{\text{final}})$ sequences, and a wave lasts as long as its longest response. Cutting $\text{KV}(S)$ by 4× lets 4× as many rollouts run per wave and shortens the step, often by more than the attention FLOPs it saves. Lesson 14.3's async schedules then decide how much of that time the trainer can hide.

## Worked example

**Qwen3-1.7B-Base's cache.** From `config.json` at `ea980cb`: 28 layers, 8 KV heads of 128. Per token: $2 \cdot 28 \cdot 8 \cdot 128 \cdot 2 = 114{,}688$ bytes in BF16. At 128K ($131{,}072$) tokens: $114{,}688 \cdot 131{,}072 = 1.50 \times 10^{10}$ bytes $= 14.0$ GiB per sequence.

**Capacity.** 80 GB HBM is 80 GiB here (the course's convention for the lab, an upper bound), 10% kept free: $72 \cdot 2^{30} = 7.73 \times 10^{10}$ bytes. Weights: $1.72 \times 10^9 \cdot 2 = 3.44 \times 10^9$ bytes. $(7.73 - 0.34) \times 10^{10} / 1.50 \times 10^{10} = 4.9$: 4 sequences at 128K. With MLA at $d_c = 512$, $d_r = 64$ the cache is $576 \cdot 28 \cdot 2 = 32{,}256$ bytes per token, 3.94 GiB per sequence, and 17 sequences fit.

**A decode step.** At $B = 8$, $S = 32{,}768$: cache bytes $8 \cdot 114{,}688 \cdot 32{,}768 = 3.01 \times 10^{10}$, plus $3.44 \times 10^9$ of weights, $3.35 \times 10^{10}$ bytes in all; at 3.35 TB/s that is 10.0 ms. FLOPs: $8 \cdot (2 \cdot 1.72 \times 10^9 + 4 \cdot 28 \cdot 2{,}048 \cdot 32{,}768) = 8 \cdot (3.4 \times 10^9 + 7.5 \times 10^9) = 8.7 \times 10^{10}$, 0.09 ms at 989 TFLOP/s. Memory-bound by a factor of 100; 800 tokens/s.

**The prefill stall.** An 8,192-token prefill costs about $8{,}192 \cdot 2 \cdot 1.72 \times 10^9 = 2.8 \times 10^{13}$ FLOPs plus attention, 36 ms at the H100's peak (the lab's roofline). A running user whose steps take 3 ms sees one 39 ms step: one stall exceeds a 10 ms TPOT objective on its own.

**An RL step.** 256 rollouts, 512-token prompts, 4,096 new tokens each, 54 GiB of HBM left after the trainer's 25.6 GiB. GQA: $\text{KV}(4{,}608) = 0.49$ GiB, so 92 fit (the lab's calculation with 10% reserve) and the step needs 3 waves; MLA at 0.14 GiB fits all 256 in one wave.

## What the evidence says

- **Decode is memory-bound at small batch: ESTABLISHED** (the roofline, measured in every serving paper; Module 2).
- **KV-cache reduction raises serving throughput: ESTABLISHED.** MLA (DeepSeek-V2: 93.3% smaller cache, 5.76× maximum generation throughput vs DeepSeek 67B; PUBLICLY DOCUMENTED); sliding windows (Gemma 3, gpt-oss: half of gpt-oss-120b's 36 layers are 128-token banded); linear hybrids (Qwen3-Next: 36 of 48 layers linear). The released-model cache sizes in the lab come from the pinned configs.
- **Chunked prefill: ESTABLISHED** (Sarathi-Serve; the default scheduling mode of current vLLM and SGLang releases, REASONABLE INDUSTRY PRACTICE to check in the engine's docs at the pinned version).
- **Disaggregated prefill and decode: PROMISING moving to ESTABLISHED** for large deployments (DistServe, Splitwise, Mooncake; company claims for production numbers). The gain depends on prompt length, model size, interconnect and the prefill/decode split, which is why the lab simulates several splits instead of quoting one number.
- **Attention design as an RL-throughput lever: INFERENCE** from the arithmetic above and REASONABLE INDUSTRY PRACTICE; open RL reports rarely isolate it. Kimi Linear and MiniMax-M1 motivate their attention designs partly by long-generation efficiency (company claims).
- **Course measurement (2026-10-07):** every simulator and roofline number in the lab is a calculation, labelled PROJECTED. Measured on the CPU: toy caches equal the formulas byte for byte for all four kinds; prefill per token was about 20–100× cheaper than decode per token, a batching effect, not the GPU's memory-bound decode.

## Common mistakes

- **Quoting KV per token without the context length.** For windowed and linear layers the per-token figure falls with $S$; say at which $S$ you measured it.
- **Counting DSA or any top-k attention as a memory saving.** It reads fewer keys per query; it stores all of them.
- **Using peak FLOPs to estimate decode time.** Decode at small batch is bounded by bytes, not FLOPs; FLOPs-based estimates are 10–100× optimistic.
- **Forgetting the trainer's memory in colocated RL.** The engine gets what is left after weights, gradients and optimizer state, and capacity falls with it.
- **Comparing schedulers on throughput alone.** Prefill-first can have the highest tokens/s and the worst TPOT; report goodput at stated SLOs.
- **Reading a simulator as a measurement.** Calibrate the efficiency factor with one real benchmark before trusting the ranking.
